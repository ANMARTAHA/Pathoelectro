01Core Idea

Fluid and Electrolyte Homeostasis

The lecture covers edema and the disorders of sodium, potassium, calcium, magnesium and phosphate: reference values and thresholds, mechanisms and causes, clinical features, diagnostic evaluation and treatment.

02Learning Points

What the student should leave with

  • Know the reference ranges and the diagnostic cut-offs for Na, K, Ca, Mg and phosphate.
  • Edema = clinically detectable ↑ interstitial fluid, driven by sodium retention; treated with Na restriction, correction of the cause and diuretics.
  • Sodium disorders reflect water relative to sodium: hypernatremia (water / hypotonic fluid loss) vs hyponatremia (excess ECF water from impaired water excretion).
  • Hyponatremia is classified by serum osmolality and by volume status; treatment depends on the type and on symptoms. Avoid overly rapid correction (osmotic demyelination).
  • Hypernatremia: correct the volume disturbance first, then the hypertonicity.
  • Potassium: hypokalemia = total-body deficit or shift into cells; hyperkalemia = intake > excretion or disturbed distribution.
  • Calcium disorders reflect the effects of parathyroid hormone and vitamin D on bone, gut and kidney.
  • Mg, K and Ca disorders can coexist; hyperphosphatemia produces hypocalcemia.
  • Treatment is chosen by severity, symptoms and clinical setting (oral vs IV, saline vs dextrose, diuretics, binders, dialysis).

03Key Definitions & Reference Values

Sodium

  • Normal 135–145 mmol/L
  • Hypernatremia serum Na >145 mmol/L (>145 mEq/L)
  • Hyponatremia serum Na <135 mmol/L
  • Mild 130–134 · Moderate 125–129 · Profound <125 mmol/L

Potassium

  • Normal 3.4–5.0 mmol/L
  • Hypokalemia serum K <3.5 mEq/L
  • Moderate 2.5–3.0 · Severe <2.5
  • Hyperkalemia serum K >5 mEq/L (>5 mmol/L)

Calcium

  • Normal total 2.12–2.60 mmol/L; ionized 1.19–1.37 mmol/L
  • Hypocalcemia total Ca <8.5 mg/dL (<2.13 mmol/L)
  • Hypercalcemia total Ca >10.5 mg/dL (>2.62 mmol/L)

Magnesium

  • Normal 0.7–1.00 mmol/L
  • Hypomagnesemia Mg <1.4 mEq/L (<0.70 mmol/L)
  • Hypermagnesemia Mg >2 mEq/L (>1 mmol/L)

Phosphate

  • Normal 0.80–1.44 mmol/L
  • Hyperphosphatemia phosphorus >4.5 mg/dL (>1.45 mmol/L)
  • Hypophosphatemia phosphorus <2 mg/dL (<0.65 mmol/L)

Edema & terms

  • Edema clinically detectable ↑ in interstitial fluid volume
  • Pedal edema limited to feet / lower legs
  • SIADH syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion
  • ATN acute tubular necrosis
  • ARDS acute respiratory distress syndrome
  • Pure-water deficit water loss without matching sodium loss (a mechanism of hypernatremia)

Tonicity (cell response)

  • Hypotonic — solute higher inside the cell; water enters; cells inflate and eventually burst
  • Isotonic — solute equal inside and outside; water in = water out
  • Hypertonic — solute lower inside the cell; water leaves; cells shrink

04Classification / Types

Types of edema

  • Peripheral — legs, feet, ankles; may involve arms
  • Lymphedema — may follow cancer treatment (surgery, radiation)
  • Pulmonary — heart failure, or noncardiogenic: infection, ARDS, toxic exposures
  • Cerebral — trauma, stroke, cancer
  • Others — e.g. macular edema

Causes of hypernatremia (mechanism)

  • Loss of hypotonic fluid — Renal: loop/thiazide diuretics, osmotic diuresis, postobstructive diuresis, diuretic phase of ATN. Nonrenal: vomiting, diarrhea, GI drainage/fistulae, sweating, burns
  • Pure-water deficit — reduced water access/intake, neurologic or psychologic disease, respiratory water loss, AVP deficiency (diabetes insipidus)
  • Hypertonic sodium gain — hypertonic electrolyte solutions, sodium bicarbonate, sodium ingestion, sodium modeling in hemodialysis
  • Water shift into cells — extreme exercise or seizures (↑ intracellular osmoles)

Classification of hyponatremia

  • Isotonic — isotonic serum osmolality
  • Hypertonic — hypertonic serum osmolality
  • Hypotonic – hypovolemic — loss of ECF volume and Na; Na loss > water loss; may occur with thiazides
  • Hypotonic – euvolemic — normal/slightly ↓ ECF Na with ↑ total body water; commonly SIADH
  • Hypotonic – hypervolemic — ↑ ECF volume with impaired renal Na/water excretion; cirrhosis, heart failure, nephrotic syndrome

Hyperkalemia — major mechanisms

  • ↑ intake
  • ↓ excretion
  • Tubular unresponsiveness to aldosterone
  • Redistribution to extracellular space

Drug-induced hypokalemia

  • Transcellular shift — tocolytic agents, theophylline, caffeine, insulin overdose
  • Enhanced renal excretion — diuretics, high-dose penicillins, mineralocorticoids, aminoglycosides, amphotericin B, cisplatin
  • Enhanced fecal elimination — laxatives, sorbitol

Hypercalcemia — mechanisms

  • ↑ bone resorption
  • ↑ GI absorption
  • ↑ renal tubular reabsorption

Hypophosphatemia — mechanisms

  • ↓ GI absorption
  • ↓ tubular reabsorption
  • Extracellular-to-intracellular redistribution

05Important Concepts

Edema

  • Develops when excess sodium is retained: impaired renal Na excretion or ↓ effective circulating volume
  • Common settings: ↓ myocardial contractility, nephrotic syndrome, cirrhosis

Hypernatremia

  • Water loss or hypotonic fluid loss; less commonly hypertonic fluid administration or sodium ingestion
  • Neurologic: weakness, lethargy, restlessness, irritability, confusion
  • Rapid onset: twitching, seizures, coma, death

Hyponatremia

  • Excess extracellular water relative to sodium, from impaired water excretion
  • Non-osmotic AVP release in hypovolemia, CHF, nephrosis, cirrhosis or SIADH
  • Severity depends on magnitude and rapidity of onset

Hypokalemia

  • Total-body K deficit or shift of K into cells
  • Causes: loop/thiazide diuretics, diarrhea, vomiting, hypomagnesemia
  • Moderate: muscle weakness, cramping, malaise, myalgia
  • Severe: arrhythmias and ECG changes

Hyperkalemia

  • Intake exceeds excretion, or transcellular distribution is disturbed
  • Often asymptomatic; palpitations and ECG changes may occur

Hypocalcemia

  • Altered effects of PTH and vitamin D on bone, gut and kidney
  • Primary causes: postoperative hypoparathyroidism, vitamin D deficiency
  • Tetany = hallmark of acute hypocalcemia; prolonged QT may occur

Hypercalcemia

  • Cancer and hyperparathyroidism = most common causes
  • Malignancy-associated: anorexia, nausea, vomiting, constipation, polyuria, polydipsia, nocturia

Hypomagnesemia

  • GI or renal disorders, several drugs; commonly associated with alcoholism
  • Neuromuscular and cardiovascular systems most affected; ventricular arrhythmias particularly important
  • May coexist with hypokalemia and hypocalcemia

Hypermagnesemia

  • Usually reduced GFR / advanced CKD
  • Other causes: Mg-containing antacids, nutrition support, eclampsia treatment, lithium, hypothyroidism, Addison disease
  • Rising Mg: sedation, hyporeflexia, coma, paralysis, respiratory depression
  • Cardiovascular effects progress from hypotension and bradycardia

Hyperphosphatemia

  • Most commonly ↓ phosphorus excretion from decreased GFR
  • Intracellular phosphate release: rhabdomyolysis, hemolysis, tumor lysis syndrome
  • Major effects: hypocalcemia and calcium-phosphate precipitation in tissues

Hypophosphatemia

  • Associated with: inadequate phosphate in parenteral nutrition, chronic alcoholism, chronic antacid use, DKA, prolonged hyperventilation
  • Severe disease affects neurologic, skeletal muscle, respiratory and cardiovascular systems

06Important Differences

Hypernatremia vs hyponatremia

  • Hyper: Na >145 — water / hypotonic fluid loss
  • Hypo: Na <135 — excess ECF water relative to Na (impaired water excretion)

Hypotonic hyponatremia by volume

  • Hypovolemic: ECF volume and Na lost; Na loss > water loss
  • Euvolemic: ECF Na normal/slightly ↓, total body water ↑; SIADH
  • Hypervolemic: ECF volume ↑, impaired renal Na/water excretion

Which test separates what (hyponatremia)

  • Serum osmolality — true hyponatremia vs pseudohyponatremia or hypertonic hyponatremia
  • Urine osmolality — impaired free-water excretion vs primary polydipsia
  • Urinary Na — hypovolemic hyponatremia vs SIADH

Which test separates what (hypernatremia)

  • Urine & plasma osmolality — nonrenal causes vs renal water loss
  • Urine output — polyuria prompts evaluation for diabetes insipidus
  • Plasma AVP — central vs nephrogenic diabetes insipidus

Central vs nephrogenic DI (treatment)

  • Central: desmopressin
  • Nephrogenic: thiazide diuretic + dietary Na restriction

Hyperkalemia interventions compared

  • Dialysis — most rapid way to lower serum K
  • IV calcium — reverses ECG changes/arrhythmias but does NOT lower K
  • Insulin + dextrose / bicarbonate / salbutamol — shift K into cells
  • Loop diuretics — ↑ renal K excretion
  • Potassium binders — suitable asymptomatic mild–moderate cases

Hyperphosphatemia binders

  • Aluminum salts, calcium carbonate
  • Non-calcium / non-aluminum: sevelamer, lanthanum carbonate, sucroferric oxyhydroxide, ferric citrate

Route of replacement

  • K: oral KCl preferred; IV for severe, symptomatic or oral-intolerant
  • Mg: IV if <1 mEq/L or symptomatic; oral if >1 mEq/L; IM only if severe/limited venous access (painful)
  • Phosphorus: oral if mild–moderate; IV if severe/symptomatic
  • Ca: calcium gluconate preferred peripherally (less irritating)

Edema types

  • Peripheral = legs, feet, ankles (± arms); pedal = feet/lower legs only

07Processes / Stages

Hypernatremia management sequence

  • 1. Recognize symptoms and identify the underlying cause
  • 2. Correct volume disturbance
  • 3. Correct hypertonicity
  • Hypovolemic: start with 0.9% saline → after stabilization replace free-water deficit with 5% dextrose or 0.45% saline

Hyponatremia management

  • Severe symptoms: 100 mL of 3% NaCl IV over 10 min, repeated up to ×3
  • Mild–moderate symptoms, low herniation risk: 3% NaCl 0.5–2 mL/kg/h
  • Throughout: avoid overly rapid correction (osmotic demyelination)

Hypocalcemia stepwise

  • Acute symptomatic → IV soluble calcium salts
  • Chronic → oral calcium
  • Add vitamin D if calcium does not normalize
  • Selected cases → recombinant human PTH

Hypercalcemia stepwise

  • Mild–moderate asymptomatic → treat cause, correct fluid/electrolytes
  • Crisis → normal saline rehydration, then loop diuretics in appropriate patients
  • Saline contraindicated → calcitonin

Severity grading

  • Hyponatremia: mild 130–134 → moderate 125–129 → profound <125
  • Hypokalemia: <3.5 → moderate 2.5–3.0 → severe <2.5

08Clinical / Practical Importance

Hypernatremia — work-up

  • Serum electrolytes (Na, K, Ca) · glucose · kidney function (urea, creatinine)
  • Urine electrolytes (Na, K) · urine & plasma osmolality
  • 24-h urine volume · plasma AVP if indicated

Hypernatremia — treatment

  • Central DI: desmopressin
  • Nephrogenic DI: thiazide + dietary Na restriction
  • Sodium overload: loop diuretics + 5% dextrose

Hyponatremia — treatment

  • SIADH: 3% saline + loop diuretic; selected cases: NaCl/urea tablets, demeclocycline, vaptans
  • Hypovolemic hypotonic: 0.9% saline
  • Hypervolemic hypotonic: 3% saline, fluid restriction, loop diuretic

Edema — treatment

  • Na restriction + correct underlying disease
  • Diuretics primary: loop (most potent) > thiazides > K-sparing

Hypokalemia — treatment

  • Mild deficit in chronic loop/thiazide use: 40–100 mEq K
  • Oral KCl preferred
  • IV K for severe, symptomatic, or oral intolerance
  • Give IV K in saline — dextrose may stimulate insulin and worsen intracellular K shift

Hyperkalemia — additional

  • Magnesium sulfate — arrhythmias from digitalis toxicity in hyperkalemic patients

Hypocalcemia — treatment

  • Acute symptomatic: IV calcium salts (gluconate peripherally)
  • Associated hypomagnesemia: Mg supplementation
  • Chronic: oral calcium ± vitamin D
  • Selected: recombinant human PTH

Hypercalcemia — treatment

  • Malignancy-associated: bisphosphonates; denosumab after suboptimal bisphosphonate response
  • Parathyroid carcinoma: cinacalcet has been used
  • Corticosteroids may ↓ GI Ca absorption in myeloma, leukemia, lymphoma, sarcoidosis, hypervitaminosis A/D

Hypomagnesemia — treatment

  • Severe / limited venous access: IM Mg (painful)
  • <1 mEq/L or symptomatic: IV Mg
  • >1 mEq/L: oral Mg (sustained-release may improve tolerance)
  • Renal insufficiency: reduce Mg dose
  • Avoid urinary K loss: amiloride, spironolactone or triamterene

Hypermagnesemia — treatment

  • IV calcium — antagonizes neuromuscular and cardiovascular effects
  • 0.45% NaCl + loop diuretic — ↑ Mg elimination with adequate renal function
  • Dialysis patients: Mg-free dialysate
  • Glucose + insulin — may promote Mg entry into cells; glucose prevents hypoglycemia

Hyperphosphatemia — treatment

  • ↓ GI absorption: phosphate binders
  • Severe symptomatic with tetany: IV calcium salts
  • ↑ renal excretion: loop diuretics or acetazolamide (carbonic anhydrase inhibitor)

Hypophosphatemia — treatment

  • Mild–moderate/asymptomatic: oral phosphorus
  • Severe/symptomatic: IV phosphorus
  • Monitor phosphorus and calcium frequently in renal dysfunction or during IV replacement
  • Vitamin D enhances intestinal and renal phosphate absorption
  • Burosumab: X-linked hypophosphatemia, adults and children ≥6 months

Severe hypophosphatemia — manifestations

  • Neurologic: irritability, weakness, numbness, paresthesias, dysarthria, confusion, seizures, coma
  • Skeletal muscle: myalgia, bone pain, weakness, potentially fatal rhabdomyolysis
  • Respiratory: respiratory muscle weakness, diaphragmatic dysfunction → acute respiratory failure
  • Others: cardiomyopathy, arrhythmias, hemolysis, ↑ infection risk
  • Chronic: osteopenia and osteomalacia

09Exam Focus

High-yield points from the lecture

  • All reference ranges and cut-offs (Na 135–145; K 3.4–5.0; total Ca 2.12–2.60; PO₄ 0.80–1.44; Mg 0.7–1.00 mmol/L).
  • Hyponatremia grades: mild 130–134, moderate 125–129, profound <125.
  • Hypokalemia: moderate 2.5–3.0, severe <2.5.
  • Five hyponatremia types, and which test separates them (serum osm, urine osm, urinary Na).
  • Four mechanisms of hypernatremia with their examples.
  • Hypernatremia: volume first, then tonicity; hypovolemic → 0.9% saline then 5% dextrose / 0.45% saline.
  • Hyponatremia: 3% NaCl 100 mL over 10 min (×3); avoid rapid correction → osmotic demyelination.
  • IV K in saline, not dextrose; oral KCl preferred.
  • Hyperkalemia: dialysis fastest; IV calcium reverses ECG but does not lower K; insulin/bicarbonate/salbutamol shift K.
  • Tetany + prolonged QT = acute hypocalcemia; calcium gluconate peripherally.
  • Hypercalcemia: cancer and hyperparathyroidism; saline → loop diuretics; bisphosphonates, denosumab, cinacalcet, calcitonin, corticosteroids by setting.
  • Hypomagnesemia: alcoholism, ventricular arrhythmias, coexists with ↓K and ↓Ca. Hypermagnesemia: CKD; IV calcium antagonizes.
  • Hyperphosphatemia: ↓GFR → hypocalcemia and Ca-phosphate precipitation; binders.
  • Severe hypophosphatemia: rhabdomyolysis, respiratory failure; burosumab for X-linked form.
